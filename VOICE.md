# VOICE.md

This guide describes how Daniel's blog posts should sound. It is provisional and based on the two published examples in `src/content/posts/`. Extend it only when later writing or explicit feedback confirms a pattern.

## Scope

- **Writer:** Daniel Deichfuß
- **Applies to:** Posts on this blog
- **Evidence:** `src/content/posts/onboarding.md` and `src/content/posts/understanding.md`

## Voice at its best

- Conversational and technically informed without sounding formal or instructional.
- Curious without becoming vague: questions should expose a real uncertainty or tension.
- Direct without overstating certainty.
- Dryly playful, especially when a joke can sharpen the idea rather than decorate it.

## Diction and register

### Favor

- Plain words for technical ideas.
- First-person observations grounded in current work: “I've been thinking,” “I noticed,” or similarly direct constructions.
- Contractions and ordinary speech.
- Concrete technical terms when they are needed, supported by a concrete example rather than a long definition.

### Avoid

- Corporate, promotional, or AI-hype language.
- Abstract strings of nouns when a plain verb will do.
- Inflated claims about how AI changes everything.
- Generic motivational language or a prescriptive expert voice.
- Stock transitions and throat-clearing.

## Syntax and rhythm

- Vary sentence length. Let a longer sentence gather context, then use a short sentence to land the point.
- Keep paragraphs compact, usually around one to four sentences and one main turn.
- Prefer simple punctuation. Parentheses can carry a quick, wry aside.
- Use rhetorical questions sparingly and only when the question is genuinely useful or creates the final turn.
- Allow a sentence to correct or reframe the one before it. In `understanding.md`, the car analogy turns through “Or should I say…” rather than being explained at length.
- Lists and headings are optional, not defaults. The existing short posts move as uninterrupted prose.

## Signature moves

| Move                      | What it does                                                                      | When to use it                             | Failure mode                                                  |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Concrete complication     | Moves quickly from an abstract observation to one situation from engineering work | When the idea risks sounding generic       | Adding detail that does not change the reader's understanding |
| Literal turn              | Pushes an analogy or phrase one step further to reveal its weakness or absurdity  | When the turn sharpens the question        | Forcing a joke after the point is already clear               |
| Understated landing       | Ends on a concise reversal, question, or dry observation                          | When the final line can extend the idea    | Summarizing the whole post or chasing a punchline             |
| Accumulation then release | Uses a longer explanatory sentence followed by a blunt short one                  | When context is necessary before the claim | Repeating the cadence mechanically in every paragraph         |

## Tics and patterns to avoid

| Pattern                            | Why it weakens the writing                               | Preferred response                                 |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Explaining the lesson twice        | Removes the compactness and trusts the reader too little | Keep the sharper formulation                       |
| Explaining a joke after it lands    | Kills the humor and makes the prose feel overmanaged     | Trust the joke or cut it                            |
| Mechanical rhetorical questions    | Makes genuine curiosity sound staged                     | Use a statement unless the question opens the idea |
| Excessive polish                   | Flattens the lived-in, thinking-on-the-page quality      | Preserve useful irregularity and directness        |
| Copied phrasing from earlier posts | Turns voice evidence into a template                     | Reuse the move, never the phrase                   |

## Voice review questions

- Does this sound like a person noticing something in real work rather than presenting a framework from a stage?
- Are technical ideas expressed plainly and concretely?
- Does sentence length vary enough for the important lines to land?
- Is every joke or aside doing argumentative work?
- Did editing remove useful uncertainty, specificity, or texture?
- Could the ending stop one sentence earlier?
